From Yellow Peril to Silicon Souls The Evolution of Techno-Orientalism in Western Speculative Fiction

How Western speculative fiction turned Asia into the future — and kept the future for itself. From the Yellow Peril to cyberpunk to the AI era's 'sympathetic Orientalism', with The Creator (2023) as the culminating case.

By Surendran and Claude (Anthropic) · A co-authored, long-form scholarly essay in seven parts · 2026

Abstract

This essay traces the career of techno-Orientalism — the Western habit of imagining Asia and Asians in the register of technology — from its roots in classical Orientalist discourse and Yellow Peril fiction, through its codification in the cyberpunk of the 1980s, its migration from Japan-anxiety to China-anxiety in the twenty-first century, and its latest mutation in the era of artificial intelligence. We argue that techno-Orientalism is best understood not as a fixed stereotype but as an adaptable representational machinery whose surface content changes with each geopolitical season while its deep grammar — aggregation of Asian particularity into an undifferentiated mass, the reduction of Asian persons to backdrop or instrument, and the denial of narrative agency — persists across every phase. The essay's central original contribution is a two-axes analysis distinguishing moral valence (who a text casts as good or evil) from representational structure (who a text treats as a subject rather than scenery). This distinction dissolves the apparent paradox by which a single film — our culminating case study is The Creator (2023) — can be attacked simultaneously as 'anti-Western' and as anti-Asian: a work may flatter Asia morally while still instrumentalising it structurally. We name this late mutation "sympathetic Orientalism" and argue that sympathetic Orientalism is still Orientalism. A second thread runs through the whole essay: the discourse's selective geography. 'Asia' in techno-Orientalist representation has meant East Asia almost exclusively; South Asia — home to the world's most populous nation and to the labour force that services the West's software infrastructure — is assigned instead to a mystic past or a hypotechnological present. We treat this sorting of Asia into temporal registers not as an omission to be corrected but as one of the discourse's constitutive rules, and track it from the exclusion-era category 'Asiatic' to the flattened "New Asia" of contemporary cinema. Part 1 lays the theoretical foundation: Edward Said's account of Orientalism as a discourse of power, the Yellow Peril archive from which techno-Orientalism inherited its imagery of the mechanical horde, the coining of the term by David Morley and Kevin Robins amid the 'Japan panic' of the early 1990s, and the fullest scholarly development of the concept by David S. Roh, Betsy Huang and Greta A. Niu. The part closes by isolating the pivotal manoeuvre on which everything later turns: the temporal inversion by which the East, long figured as the West's primitive past, was refigured as its dehumanised future.

Keywords: techno-Orientalism; Orientalism; Yellow Peril; speculative fiction; South Asia; representation

Part OneTheoretical Foundations and Historical Roots

There is a peculiar consistency in the way Western speculative fiction has furnished its futures. When the genre wants to signal that the world to come will be crowded, vertical, rain-slicked and morally ambiguous, it reaches — with a reliability that ought to puzzle us more than it does — for Asian faces, Asian scripts, Asian streetscapes. Neon ideograms glow above the arcologies; noodle vendors serve the detective in the acid rain; the corporation that owns the future has a Japanese or Chinese name. The convention is so familiar that it passes for neutral world-building. It is not neutral. It has a history, a politics and a grammar, and the name scholarship has settled on for it is techno-Orientalism: in the definition given by David S. Roh, Betsy Huang and Greta A. Niu, "the phenomenon of imagining Asia and Asians in hypo- or hypertechnological terms in cultural productions and political discourse"[1]. This essay reconstructs where that phenomenon came from, how it has mutated across four decades of film, fiction and games, and what it has become in the age of artificial intelligence — when, we will argue, it has learned to smile.

Any such reconstruction must begin with Edward Said. Orientalism (1978) supplied the analytical vocabulary without which the 'techno-' compound is unintelligible, and its central claims bear restating with some care, because the later argument of this essay — that a text can be Orientalist while being morally generous to the Orient — is already latent in them. Said's subject was not Asia but the West's idea of Asia: Orientalism as "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient"[2]. Three theses matter here. First, Orientalism is a discourse in the Foucauldian sense — a self-sustaining system of statements, images, scholarly institutions and administrative practices that produces 'the Orient' as an object of knowledge, rather than a mere collection of individual prejudices that better information might correct[2]. The Orientalist does not simply misdescribe the East; he constructs an East about which certain things can be said and others cannot, and the construction does real work in the world, licensing conquest, exclusion and tutelage. Second, the discourse has a remarkably stable conceptual core. Said distinguished latent Orientalism — the almost unconscious substrate of assumptions about the East as timeless, sensual, despotic, irrational, feminine, incapable of self-government — from manifest Orientalism, the explicit statements, policies and representations in which that substrate surfaces at any given historical moment[2]. The manifest content changes with the century; the latent grammar barely moves. This distinction is the direct ancestor of the two-axes analysis we will develop in Part 4, and it is worth flagging the inheritance now: Said had already seen that the surface of Orientalist representation is negotiable while its structure is conserved. Third — and least comfortable for the liberal conscience — Orientalism does not require hostility. Said insisted that the discourse embraced its objects of fascination, sympathy and even love; the Romantic poets who swooned over the mystic East and the philologists who devoted their lives to Sanskrit were doing Orientalism as surely as the colonial administrators[2]. Affection, in this analysis, is not an alibi. A tradition that can 'other' through admiration in 1820 will prove perfectly capable of 'othering' through sympathy in 2023.

Classical Orientalism, then, positioned the East on a temporal axis: behind. The Orient was the West's own past — cradle of civilisation, museum of origins, a place where time had congealed. Its people were ancient, unchanging, picturesque; whatever dynamism history required would be supplied from outside. Nowhere was the operation more explicit than in British India, classical Orientalism's largest laboratory. The same colonial culture that founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784) and exalted Sanskrit as the venerable ancestor of European tongues ruled the living speakers of its descendants as incapable of self-government; admiration for India's antiquity and contempt for India's present were two settings of one instrument. Macaulay's notorious Minute on Indian Education (1835) — with its verdict that "a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia" — merely made the temporal sentence explicit: India's greatness was archival, finished, a past to be curated by others, while its future would be written in English[3]. The assignment proved remarkably durable. India was fixed in the Western imagination as spiritual antiquity — the land of sages, scriptures and timeless wisdom — and, as we shall see, this original sentencing never underwent the inversion that would later transfer East Asia to the future. Hold that asymmetry in mind; it becomes a load-bearing datum in Part 3. It is against this backdrop that the late nineteenth century's Yellow Peril panic marks a genuinely strange development, because it introduced, for the first time, the possibility that the East might be ahead — or at least coming — and it clothed that possibility in the imagery of mass, machinery and swarm from which techno-Orientalism would later assemble its aesthetic.

The panic had a political and demographic substrate before it had a literature. In the United States, Chinese labourers — recruited in their thousands for the goldfields and the transcontinental railroad — became the target of an escalating exclusion movement that culminated in the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the first federal law to bar immigration on explicitly racial-national grounds[4]. The rhetoric of the exclusion campaigns is instructive: the Chinese worker was figured not as an individual competitor but as a quantity — tireless, undifferentiated, needless of rest or wage, an economic machine in human form whose very efficiency was the threat. Historians John Kuo Wei Tchen and Dylan Yeats, surveying this archive, show how thoroughly anti-Asian fear was, from the beginning, a fear of aggregated and mechanised labour rather than of persons[4]. The trope that cyberpunk would one day render in chrome — the Asian body as biological robot — was forged in the pamphlets of the 1870s.

The literature followed. In 1895 Kaiser Wilhelm II gave the anxiety its name and its icon, popularising the phrase 'the yellow peril' (die gelbe Gefahr) and commissioning an allegorical engraving — Europe's nations as armoured valkyries, warned by the Archangel Michael against a gathering storm from the East — which he distributed to the courts of Europe with evangelical enthusiasm[5]. Three years later M. P. Shiel's invasion novel The Yellow Danger (1898) supplied the genre template: a brilliant, patient, implacable Chinese mastermind, Dr Yen How, orchestrating the conquest of Europe through sheer demographic mass[6]. Jack London's short story "The Unparalleled Invasion" (1910) carried the logic to its genocidal conclusion — a future war in which the Western powers, unable to defeat China's numbers, exterminate its population with biological weapons, an outcome the story narrates with disquieting equanimity[7]. And in 1913 Sax Rohmer gathered the whole tradition into a single durable icon: Dr Fu-Manchu, "the yellow peril incarnate in one man", a villain who fused ancient Oriental cruelty with cutting-edge scientific genius[8]. Christopher Frayling's study of the Fu Manchu phenomenon demonstrates how completely this figure saturated twentieth-century popular culture, fixing the template of the Asian scientist-despot for a hundred years of pulp, film and propaganda[9].

Pause on the structure of these fictions, because their deep grammar outlived their surface politics. First, aggregation: the Yellow Peril text almost never fears an Asian individual — it fears the mass, the swarm, the four hundred million. Where an individual appears at all, he is either the mastermind who is the mass's will personified (Yen How, Fu-Manchu) or a faceless unit within it. Ordinary Asian personhood — the middle register in which most human beings actually live, neither demonic genius nor anonymous horde — is precisely what the genre cannot represent. Second, instrumentality: the Asian figure is imagined as a means — of labour, of conquest, of production — rather than as an end; the exclusion-era caricature of the coolie who works without tiring is already the caricature of a machine that merely resembles a man. Third, the fusion of the archaic and the technical: Fu-Manchu commands both ancient poisons and modern laboratories; the peril is frightening precisely because it welds Oriental 'timelessness' to Occidental instruments. The Yellow Peril, in other words, invented the compound that techno-Orientalism would standardise: Asia as a technology — powerful, efficient, inscrutable and soulless[4][9].

The peril, moreover, was never only 'yellow' in the narrow East Asian sense, and the breadth of the category is itself instructive. On the North American Pacific coast the panic extended seamlessly to South Asian migrants — mostly Punjabi Sikhs, indiscriminately labelled 'Hindoos' — who were driven from Bellingham, Washington, by a white mob in 1907 amid newspaper warnings of a 'Hindu invasion'; a decade later the Immigration Act of 1917 drew an 'Asiatic Barred Zone' on the map of the world, excluding immigrants from a territory stretching from the Middle East through India to the Pacific islands; and in United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind (1923) the Supreme Court ruled that a South Asian, whatever the ethnologists said about 'Caucasian' classification, was not white in 'the understanding of the common man' and therefore could not naturalise[10]. The legal category 'Asiatic', in other words, aggregated Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Indian into a single excludable mass half a century before any Hollywood production designer did the same — the flattening of Asia's internal differences that we will meet again in The Creator's "New Asia" is as old as the panic itself. Yet within the shared category, the imagery was already sorted into registers. The East Asian was figured as economic machine and demographic swarm — the proto-technological register; the South Asian was figured as religious contagion, fanaticism and unassimilable mysticism — the turbaned 'Hindu' menacing not the factory but the faith[10][4]. The mechanical future, when it was later distributed, would be distributed along exactly this fault line: the machine imagery was reserved for East Asia from the beginning, and India's exclusion from the technological imaginary was written into the discourse at the moment of its foundation.

It remained for the late twentieth century to give the compound its name, and the occasion was economic. By the 1980s Japan — occupied, demilitarised and 'tutored' by the United States a generation earlier — had become the West's principal industrial rival: its cars, semiconductors and consumer electronics displacing American products, its corporations buying Manhattan landmarks and Hollywood studios, its management techniques anatomised in anxious bestsellers. The pupil had not merely caught the master; in the domain that the West regarded as its own essence — technological modernity — the pupil appeared to be ahead. The cultural processing of this shock is the subject of David Morley and Kevin Robins's chapter "Techno-Orientalism: Japan Panic" in Spaces of Identity (1995), the text that coined the term[11]. Their argument deserves careful summary. Western identity, they observe, had long been secured by a simple equation: the West is the future, the rest are the past. Japan's ascendancy broke the equation. "If the future is technological, and if technology has become 'Japanised', then the syllogism would suggest that the future is now Japanese too"[11]. Something had to give — and what gave was the value attached to the future. If the future could no longer be denied to Japan, it could be emptied: the Japanese future was refigured as a future without soul. Morley and Robins document the resulting image-repertoire in the journalism, trade rhetoric and popular culture of the period: the Japanese as "cold, impersonal and machine-like", a people of imitation without creativity, efficiency without feeling, surface without interior — an "authoritarian culture" whose technological mastery proved not their humanity but their lack of it[11]. Where the classical Orientalist had said they are behind us, and that is why they are lesser, the techno-Orientalist now said they are ahead of us, and that is why they are lesser. The deficit merely changed address: from technics to soul.

This is the manoeuvre we shall call the temporal inversion, and it is the hinge of the entire history this essay narrates. Its elegance, as an ideological operation, lies in what it conserves. The hierarchy is preserved — the West remains the seat of authentic humanity — while every empirical fact that might have threatened the hierarchy is absorbed as further evidence for it. Japanese excellence in robotics? A robotic people. Japanese efficiency? Inhuman discipline. Japanese aesthetics? Blank, affectless surface. The inversion converts achievement into symptom. And it does so by quietly redefining the human itself: humanity is relocated from capability (where the West no longer holds the monopoly) to interiority — soul, feeling, spontaneity, 'real' individuality — where the West can simply assert possession, since interiority, unlike semiconductor yield, admits no audit. Said's latent Orientalism had held that the Oriental is not fully an individual; the techno-Orientalist inversion preserved exactly this axiom while reversing the temporal sign under which it appears[2][11]. The anthropologist Johannes Fabian gave the underlying operation its most precise name: the "denial of coevalness" — the discursive refusal to allow the observed culture to inhabit the same present as the observer[12]. Classical Orientalism denied coevalness backwards, exiling Asia to the past; techno-Orientalism denies it forwards, exiling Asia to the future; what neither mode can tolerate is Asian contemporaneity — Asians as ordinary inhabitants of the shared now, which is the tense in which actual persons live, choose and act. And the inversion, note, was applied selectively. Japan — and later China and Korea — were reassigned from past to future; India was not. The South Asian remained under the original sentence of mystic antiquity even as the East Asian was issued the new sentence of soulless futurity: two exiles from the present, in opposite temporal directions, issued by the same court. The continuity beneath the reversal is the first major piece of evidence for this essay's larger claim: that techno-Orientalism is a machinery whose settings change and whose function does not.

Two decades after Morley and Robins, the edited collection Techno-Orientalism: Imagining Asia in Speculative Fiction, History, and Media (2015), assembled by Roh, Huang and Niu, gave the concept its fullest scholarly development and its now-standard definition, quoted above[1]. Three of the collection's refinements will matter repeatedly in what follows. First, the hypo/hyper pairing: techno-Orientalism is not a single stereotype but an oscillation, imagining Asians as simultaneously hypertechnological (masters of the machine, avatars of the future) and hypotechnological (mere imitators, cheap labour, incapable of true creation) — and the two poles, far from contradicting one another, cooperate, since both deny the Asian subject ordinary creative personhood[1]. A figure may be all machine-competence and no inventive soul; the sweatshop and the cyborg are one image seen from two angles. Second, the editors stress that techno-Orientalism is future-oriented: where Said's Orientalism projected its Orient into an imagined past, techno-Orientalism projects it into an imagined future, which makes speculative fiction — the genre licensed to build futures — its natural habitat and its most efficient vector[1]. Third, the collection insists that the discourse is not confined to fiction: it circulates through trade journalism, policy discourse and political rhetoric, shaping how Western publics interpret real Asian economies and real Asian persons[1]. The stakes of getting the analysis right are therefore not merely literary-critical. Representational grammars travel; the swarm imagery of the 1880s exclusion campaigns returned almost verbatim in twentieth-century wartime propaganda and, as we shall see in Part 4, returns again in twenty-first-century commentary on Chinese artificial intelligence.

Two qualifications should be entered before the kit is closed, one from within Asia and one concerning gender. The Japanese media theorist Ueno Toshiya, writing in the late 1990s, observed that techno-Orientalism functions as a kind of distorting mirror suspended between Japan and the West: the West gazes into it and sees its own technological future wearing an Asian face, while Japan, gazing back, is tempted to costume itself in the very stereotype — high-tech, hyper-efficient, post-human — that the West has prepared for it[13]. The mirror image matters because it warns us against treating techno-Orientalism as a purely one-directional imposition; from Ghost in the Shell to state-branded 'Cool Japan', Asian cultural producers have at times inhabited, monetised and subverted the trope, a complication Part 3 will take up under the heading of self-Orientalism. The second qualification is that the Yellow Peril archive was gendered from the outset. The Page Act of 1875 — which effectively barred Chinese women from the United States on the presumption of prostitution — preceded the Exclusion Act by seven years, and the fiction that followed sorted Asian femininity into a matching pair of instruments: the 'dragon lady', seduction weaponised, and the 'lotus blossom', submission perfected[4]. Neither is a person; both are apparatus. The relevance for our purposes is structural rather than antiquarian: when techno-Orientalist cinema later populates its futures with Asian-featured androids, gynoids and holographic companions — objects designed for service, desire and disposal — it draws on a gendered template in which the Asian body had already been imagined as a compliant technology for a century[1][4].

The hypo/hyper oscillation also allows us to state, with some precision, where South Asia sits in the discourse — a question the scholarship itself has largely neglected, its canon running from Fu-Manchu to Blade Runner to Arasaka with barely a glance south of the Himalaya. The neglect is not ours to repeat, least of all now that India has overtaken China as the world's most populous nation and functions, quite literally, as the software back office of the West. Notice the shape of India's presence in the Western technological imaginary: it is almost entirely hypo. The call-centre voice mimicking an American accent at 3 a.m.; the outsourced coder; the H-1B engineer; the 'cyber-coolie' — a term that circulates in the labour-studies literature with grim aptness, welding the nineteenth-century figure of aggregated Asian labour directly to the fibre-optic cable[1]. Indian technological labour is everywhere in the Western present and nowhere in the Western future: no Western studio sets its neon dystopia in Mumbai or Bengaluru; the megacity of the speculative imagination speaks Japanese, Chinese, Korean — never Hindi, Tamil or Malayalam. India is permitted to service the machine but not to be the machine, just as East Asia is permitted to be the machine but not to own its soul. These are not two prejudices but one grammar conjugated in two tenses — the mystic past and the soulless future, with the productive Asian present unrepresentable in either register. Part 3 will develop this argument in full; it is registered here because it follows directly from the theoretical kit just assembled, and because it shows that the kit's reach is wider than its builders assumed.

One further conceptual instrument should be added to the kit before we turn to the texts themselves, because the existing scholarship gestures at it without quite isolating it. Call it backdrop-isation: the operation by which a culture is converted into scenery — present everywhere in a text's world as signage, cuisine, crowd and mood, while absent from the text's structure of persons. Backdrop-isation is distinct from villainisation and compatible with affection; a text may love its Asian backdrop, render it in loving detail, mourn its suffering — and still assign every consequential choice, every interior conflict, every arc of growth to non-Asian subjects. It is, we will argue, the load-bearing operation of techno-Orientalism in every phase: the Yellow Peril aggregated Asians into a horde; cyberpunk aggregated them into a skyline; the sympathetic mode of the 2020s aggregates them into a congregation of gentle victims. The moral colouration differs extravagantly; the aggregation is identical. Distinguishing the colouration from the operation — the valence from the structure — is the analytical thread that will guide this essay to its conclusion, and it is why the theoretical groundwork of this Part has dwelt so long on Said's insight that Orientalism survives its own philias as comfortably as its phobias[2].

We are now equipped. The conceptual inheritance runs: from Said, the understanding of Orientalism as a durable discourse with a stable latent grammar and a changeable manifest surface, hospitable to sympathy as well as contempt[2]; from the Yellow Peril archive, the concrete image-repertoire of aggregation, instrumentality and the archaic-technical fusion — an 'Asiatic' category that aggregated East and South Asia in law while already sorting them into different registers of menace[4][6][7][8][9][10]; from Morley and Robins, the temporal inversion that turned East Asia from the West's past into its soulless future[11]; from Fabian, the recognition that both the old sentence and the new one are denials of coevalness — exiles from the shared present, in opposite directions[12]; from Roh, Huang and Niu, the hypo/hyper oscillation and the recognition that speculative fiction is the discourse's principal engine[1]. What the inheritance amounts to is a machine for producing Asian futures without Asian subjects — and, in its South Asian application, Asian labour without an Asian future. In Part 2 we watch that machine acquire its definitive twentieth-century styling: the neon-drenched, rain-slicked, Japanese-inflected megacity of Blade Runner and Neuromancer, in which the aesthetic conventions of four decades of Western science fiction — and the argument of this essay — were quite literally set in place.

Part TwoThe Classic Cyberpunk Era: Japan Panic and the Codification of the Aesthetic

Part 1 assembled the theoretical kit: Said's durable discourse with its changeable surface and conserved structure; the Yellow Peril's grammar of aggregation, instrumentality and the archaic-technical fusion; the temporal inversion by which East Asia was reassigned from the West's past to its soulless future; Fabian's denial of coevalness as the operation underlying both sentences; and the hypo/hyper oscillation that lets the discourse hold 'masters of technology' and 'incapable of true creation' in a single thought. Part 2 watches this machinery produce its masterpiece. Between roughly 1979 and 1992 — between, let us say, the publication of Ezra Vogel's Japan as Number One and Michael Crichton's Rising Sun — the United States passed through an episode of economic dread whose cultural precipitate was the most durable aesthetic convention in modern science fiction: the neon-drenched, rain-slicked, Asian-signed megacity of cyberpunk. The convention was born in two texts, Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982) and William Gibson's Neuromancer (1984), and the burden of this Part is to show precisely how those texts converted a trade panic into a visual and narrative grammar — and what that grammar did, and did not do, with Asian persons.

The economic history first, because the aesthetic is unintelligible without it. Through the 1970s and 1980s Japan's export machine moved methodically up the value chain — textiles, steel, ships, automobiles, consumer electronics, semiconductors — and at each rung displaced American producers who had assumed their position was a fact of nature. The oil shocks rewarded Japanese fuel efficiency; Detroit haemorrhaged; by the mid-1980s Japanese firms led the world in memory-chip production, and the technology that Silicon Valley regarded as its birthright appeared to be passing to Tokyo. The American response oscillated between the study of Japan and the fear of it — the two moods, as we saw with the hypo/hyper oscillation, being phases of a single obsession. The studious mood produced a shelf of management literature of which Vogel's Japan as Number One: Lessons for America (1979) was the emblem: Japan as the better-run society, to be emulated in its schooling, its planning, its industrial discipline[14]. The fearful mood produced the imagery of invasion and purchase: the Plaza Accord and the semiconductor disputes; congressmen smashing a Toshiba radio with sledgehammers on the Capitol lawn in 1987; the Mitsubishi Estate purchase of Rockefeller Center and Sony's purchase of Columbia Pictures in 1989, which put a Japanese corporate name on two icons of the American imaginary in a single year. When the politician Ishihara Shintarō and the Sony founder Morita Akio published The Japan That Can Say No (1989; English translation 1991), an unauthorised rendering circulated in Washington like a captured war document[15]. And in 1992 Crichton's Rising Sun — a bestselling thriller whose Japanese businessmen are patient, collective, sexually predatory and two moves ahead of every American in the room — carried the panic to the airport bookstall, complete with an appended bibliography, as though the novel were a briefing[16]. The affective structure of the moment, as Morley and Robins diagnosed it, was precisely the one the temporal inversion exists to manage: the intolerable suspicion that the future was no longer American[11].

Nor was the panic confined to policy circles and thrillers; by the late 1980s it was ambient in Hollywood at every register of prestige. Gung Ho (1986) played the Japanese takeover of an American car plant for anxious comedy; Die Hard (1988) housed its all-American ordeal in the Los Angeles tower of the Nakatomi Corporation, opening with a Japanese executive genially conceding that Pearl Harbor had failed but cassette decks had succeeded; Black Rain (1989) sent its American detective into an Osaka shot exactly like a Blade Runner set, all steam, neon and industrial night; Back to the Future Part II (1989) equipped its 2015 with a Japanese boss video-calling to fire Marty McFly; RoboCop 3 (1993) simply made its villain a zaibatsu. The examples could be multiplied, and their spread matters analytically: the same years produced comic, thrilling, dystopian and satirical variations on one proposition — the Japanese are buying the future — which is precisely what it looks like when a representation stops being an authored statement and becomes an atmosphere, a default any genre can breathe. It is against this saturated background that the two canonical texts did their more durable work: the others recycled the anxiety; Blade Runner and Neuromancer gave it a form that could outlive it.

Blade Runner arrived in the middle of this, and its genius — the reason it, rather than any of its pulp contemporaries, became the template — lies in how completely it translated the economic anxiety into atmosphere. Scott's Los Angeles of 2019 is not conquered by Asia; it is saturated by it. The famous opening shots descend from a hellscape of refinery flares into streets where the rain never stops and the crowd is visibly, insistently East Asian: noodle bars, paper umbrellas glowing with neon tubing, hawkers, ideograms stacked storey upon storey[17]. Above it all floats the film's presiding icon, the gigantic video geisha on the advertising blimp and tower — serene, white-faced, endlessly swallowing her pill and smiling. The production record removes any doubt about the deliberateness of the coding: Scott, his special-effects supervisor David Dryer recalled, wanted "a bunch of phony oriental commercials where geisha girls are doing unhealthy things", and the design team built the street look from a memory of Hong Kong and Tokyo neon[18]. Philip K. Dick's source novel, set in a depopulated San Francisco, contains none of this; the Asianisation of the future city was the film's own contribution, drawn directly from the ambient Japan-panic of its moment[17]. What the geisha means is hardly obscure. She is the Orient as pure surface — traditional femininity fused with advertising technology, watching over a city she does not inhabit as a person; seductive, unreadable, selling something. She is Morley and Robins's argument rendered as production design: the future has an Asian face, and the face is a screen[11].

But the decisive fact about Blade Runner, for our purposes, is structural, and it is best seen by asking a simple question: what does the film's Asian presence do? The answer is: it serves, it decorates, and it manufactures — but it does not decide. The crowd is scenery. The sushi master takes Deckard's order; a Cambodian street vendor identifies a snake scale under a microscope; the eye-maker Chew, shivering in his freezing laboratory among the disembodied organs he grows, supplies the replicants' eyes — "I just do eyes," he protests, "just eyes"[17]. The line is almost too perfect an allegory. Asian labour manufactures the very organ of vision, but seeing — judging, empathising, deciding who is human — belongs exclusively to others. The film's central moral drama, the question of whether manufactured beings have souls, is conducted entirely among white principals: Deckard, Rachael, Batty, Tyrell. Not one Asian character is admitted to the plot's structure of persons; the film poses the question 'what is human?' in a city built of Asian bodies without once posing it of them. Note carefully — and this is the seed of the two-axes analysis that will flower in Part 4 — that none of this is villainisation. Blade Runner has no Asian villain; its Asia is not morally coded at all. The film's moral valence towards Asia is neutral, even affectionate in its fascination; its representational structure assigns Asia to backdrop without exception. Valence and structure have already come apart, forty years before The Creator — the difference being that in 1982 nobody thought to praise the film for its kindness to the people in its scenery[17].

Neuromancer performed the same operation in prose, and added the crucial extension: it orientalised not just the city but cyberspace itself. Gibson's novel opens under a Chiba City sky "the color of television, tuned to a dead channel", in a Japanese port town where the washed-up console cowboy Case haunts the bars of Ninsei, seeking black-market Japanese neurosurgery to repair what was burned out of him — Night City, the narrator tells us, "like a deranged experiment in social Darwinism, designed by a bored researcher who kept one thumb permanently on the fast-forward button"[19]. The simile deserves a second look: the Asian city as an experiment run at inhuman speed — accelerated, instrumental, observed from outside — is the temporal inversion in miniature. The novel's economy is Japanese: the tech is Hosaka and Ono-Sendai; the underworld is Yakuza; the corporate order is the zaibatsu, described in a phrase that distils a century of aggregation-anxiety into biology — the multinationals as "hives with cybernetic memories", organisms whose individual members are as replaceable as cells[19]. Its most accomplished Asian figure is Hideo, the Tessier-Ashpool family's cloned ninja retainer: literally serialised, literally owned, the Yellow Peril's faceless horde compressed into a single elegant instrument[19]. Around these materials Gibson builds the sensation that made the book canonical: the Western protagonist's exhilarating, dangerous navigation of a space that is dense, foreign, vertiginous and Asian-signed. Wendy Hui Kyong Chun has given this sensation its sharpest analysis under the name "high-tech Orientalism": cyberpunk, she argues, stages the disorientation of a West that no longer controls the technological world, and then converts that vulnerability into pleasure — the reader enjoys being lost in the Oriental data-city because the genre guarantees the Western navigator's mastery in the end[20]. Timothy Yu presses the point to its conclusion: in cyberpunk, "fantasies of Asia become the enabling fictions of postmodernity" — the Orient is the space the West requires in order to imagine its own future at all, which is why the future must be Asian-styled and cannot be Asian-owned[21]. Asia provides the setting, the hardware, the texture, the threat; the subjectivity moving through it, the meaning extracted from it, remain Western property. Gibson himself, two decades later, said the quiet part with disarming candour: "modern Japan simply was cyberpunk" — Japan was not being imagined, it was being used; the country had become, in his phrase, the global imagination's "default setting for the future"[22].

It is worth pausing, as we did with the Yellow Peril texts, to insist on what this grammar is not. Neither Scott nor Gibson wrote anti-Japanese tracts; both were, on any ordinary reading, fascinated admirers, and Gibson's Japan in particular is rendered with an enthusiast's relish. Nor is the point that Japanese economic power was imaginary — it was real, epochal, and Japan's own cultural industries, as Ueno's mirror predicted, promptly recognised themselves in cyberpunk's mirror and elaborated the aesthetic from within, a feedback loop Part 3 will examine[13]. The point is that the representational settlement these texts codified — Asia supplies the future's mise-en-scène; the West supplies its protagonists — was accepted so smoothly, reproduced so automatically, that it became invisible as a choice. Jane Chi Hyun Park calls the resulting convention "oriental style": Asianness as a detachable visual register connoting technological futurity, available to any Hollywood production that wants its future to look futuristic, entirely independent of any Asian character being present[23]. That detachability is the key mechanism of codification. Within a decade the style had floated free of its original referent: the Japan-panic subsided with Japan's asset bubble after 1990, but the neon ideograms did not come down. They persisted in Akira's Neo-Tokyo re-imported through Western fandom, in the Chinatown futures of a hundred straight-to-video imitations, in The Matrix's Hong Kong choreography and green cascades of code — which are, when one looks, raining ideograms: the Orient dissolved into pure signal[23][20]. An anxiety had become a style; a style, unlike an anxiety, requires no object. It only requires reuse. This is how a representational structure outlives the geopolitics that built it, and it is why, when a new Asian power rose in the 2000s, the grammar was already waiting, fully formed, needing only to swap its signage from Japanese to Chinese[1].

And India? Here the thread laid down in Part 1 yields its first dividend, because the cyberpunk decade is the thread's perfect natural experiment. The very years in which Western SF was furnishing its future with East Asian faces were the years in which India's software industry was actually being born: Infosys founded in 1981, a year before Blade Runner; the liberalisation of 1991 opening the outsourcing floodgates; by the Y2K remediation boom of the late 1990s, Indian engineers were quite literally debugging the West's future for it. If the techno-Orientalist imagination tracked technological reality, Bangalore should have joined Chiba City in the genre's atlas. It did not. Not one canonical cyberpunk future is Indian-signed; the genre that could imagine Rastafarian space stations and cloned ninja could not imagine an Indian programmer. The reason follows directly from Part 1's register-sorting: India had been sentenced to mystic antiquity, and the sentence held. Where East Asia's real technological achievement was processed into hypertechnological fantasy, India's real technological achievement was simply not processed at all — absorbed instead into the hypo register of invisible service labour, the back office that by definition never appears on screen[1][12]. East Asia was over-represented as the future and denied the present; India was denied both. The machine, as we said, conjugates one grammar in two tenses — and in the 1980s the future tense was reserved, exclusively, for the Pacific.

By 1992 or so, then, the codification was complete, and we can state exactly what had been fixed in place. A visual lexicon: neon ideograms, permanent rain, street food, holographic geishas, corporate towers with Asian names — a portable kit for signifying 'the future'[17][23]. A narrative settlement: Asian-saturated worlds, Western-centred stories; Asia as venue, atmosphere, hardware and occasionally threat, but not as protagonist[21]. An affective mode: fascination without individuation — the camera lingers on Asian surfaces and never follows an Asian person home[20]. And a proof, already available to anyone who looked, that all of this could coexist with moral neutrality and even with love: the structure required no hostility to operate, only reuse. What remained open, as the Japan panic faded, was the question of content. The grammar stood ready; whose anxiety would fill it next? The answer, of course, was China — a transfer that would bring new intensities, because China, unlike bubble-era Japan, was a strategic rival as well as an economic one. And a second, unprecedented development was stirring: the objects of the representation — Japanese animators, Korean film-makers, Chinese novelists, diasporic Asian writers — were beginning to seize the apparatus and turn it, with varying mixtures of subversion and complicity, upon themselves.

Part ThreeThe Migration to China-Anxiety, and the Other Side of the Mirror

Part 2 ended with a grammar standing empty. The Japan panic had subsided with the Tokyo asset bubble, but the representational machinery it had built — the Asian-signed future city, the settlement of Asia-as-venue and West-as-protagonist, the affective mode of fascination without individuation — persisted as pure style, awaiting a tenant. The 2000s supplied one. China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001 opened two decades in which the century's defining economic fact was Chinese manufacturing, Chinese scale and, latterly, Chinese technology; and Western speculative fiction, with almost mechanical obedience, re-let the premises. The signage changed from Japanese to Chinese; the anxiety acquired a harder geopolitical edge, since China, unlike America's bubble-era client Japan, was a strategic rival with its own political order; and the grammar itself — this is the continuity to hold onto — carried over intact. But the same two decades produced something the classic era never had to reckon with: the people on the receiving end of the representation began, at scale, to answer it. Asian and Asian-diasporic writers, film-makers and game designers took up the techno-Orientalist apparatus from the other side — sometimes to subvert it, sometimes to sell it back, sometimes to build futures of their own in which Asians are, at last, simply persons. This Part follows both movements, and then turns to the case the scholarship keeps failing to see: India, whose millions of actual technology workers remained locked in the hypo register even as fictional East Asian futures multiplied.

Begin with the Western corpus, and with its most instructive specimen. Joss Whedon's Firefly (2002) imagines a twenty-sixth century in which "the two great superpowers, America and China, fused to form the central federal government": the Sino-American Alliance. Its characters curse fluently in Mandarin; its corridors are hung with calligraphy and paper lanterns; its costume and set dressing perform a Sino-American fusion with evident affection. And across fourteen episodes and a feature film, the series contrives to include no Asian character of any consequence whatsoever[24]. The paradox is so clean it reads like an experimental design: a future constitutionally half-Chinese, in which Chinese people do not appear. Critics registered the strangeness almost immediately — Leigh Adams Wright's aptly titled essay "Asian Objects in Space" observed that the show's Asia is present entirely as things: language, fabric, food, décor, detached from any Asian body[25]. Firefly thus performs backdrop-isation in its purest laboratory form, stripped even of the crowds that populated Blade Runner's streets: the culture is fully absorbed, the people fully deleted. And note again the axes: there is no hostility here — the show plainly likes its Chinese furnishings; the moral valence of the Asian element is warmly positive. The structure is annihilating. One begins to see why the sympathetic mutation of Part 4 should have surprised nobody.

The decade's other landmarks refine the pattern rather than break it. Rian Johnson's Looper (2012) sets part of its action in a future Shanghai — a co-production concession, the Chinese city substituted for the script's original Paris — and gives the substitution its thesis statement in dialogue: "I'm from the future," the gangster tells the young protagonist. "You should go to China"[26]. The future, as a piece of real estate, has been formally reassigned. The Wachowskis' and Tom Tykwer's Cloud Atlas (2012) supplies the era's richest and most damning text: its 2144 segment, Neo-Seoul, is a totalitarian corporate dystopia whose service class consists of fabricants — genetically manufactured Asian women, cloned in their thousands, worked in a fast-food catacomb and then slaughtered and recycled into nutrient for the next generation[27]. As an image, the fabricant is the entire techno-Orientalist inheritance rendered literal: aggregation (she is one of thousands of identical units), instrumentality (she is manufactured for service and consumed as raw material), the gendered template of the compliant Asian female body (Part 1's lotus blossom, industrialised). The segment even seems half-aware of its own critique — Sonmi-451, the fabricant who attains consciousness, delivers the film's humanist credo. Yet the production undid whatever the text intended: the segment's heroic male lead, an ostensibly Korean revolutionary, was played by Jim Sturgess in prosthetic eyefolds, alongside other white actors in what the Media Action Network for Asian Americans bluntly called yellowface — while noting that the one genuinely stereotype-busting Asian male role in a decade of Hollywood SF had thus been given to a white Englishman[28]. The film, in other words, could imagine mass-producing Asian women as literal objects, but could not imagine an Asian man as a subject even when the script demanded one. Stephen Hong Sohn's formulation for the era's default equation — the "Alien/Asian", the racialised figure who stands in for the technological non-human as such — was never better illustrated[29].

That equation — Asian body, machine being — became the period's signature casting logic, and it is worth pausing on its most elegant instance. In Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014), the Turing-test drama is conducted between three white principals: the genius, the tester and Ava, the android whose personhood is the film's question. But there is a fourth figure in the house: Kyoko, an earlier-model android built as a silent East Asian servant-concubine, who cooks, dances, submits and does not speak, and whose disposal occasions no grief in the film's economy of sympathy[30]. The film thus reproduces, presumably without design, the precise structure we traced in Blade Runner: the question 'can the machine be a person?' is asked urgently of the white-featured machine and not asked at all of the Asian-featured one. Personhood is the plot when the candidate is white; furniture is the default when the candidate is Asian. The model-minority logic embedded here should be made explicit, because it connects the casting convention to the social stereotype: the model minority is praised as diligent, uncomplaining, technically able and interchangeable — which is to say, praised as a good machine — and the android roles simply literalise the compliment. The Yellow Peril's coolie-automaton and the honour-roll model minority are, as Part 1 argued of the hypo/hyper poles, one figure in two moral lightings. By the time CD Projekt Red's Cyberpunk 2077 (2020) built its open world around the Arasaka Corporation — a Japanese zaibatsu of feudal ruthlessness, complete with restored-from-the-dead patriarch — the whole kit had become period furniture: a Polish studio, adapting an American tabletop game from 1988, faithfully reproducing the Japan-panic iconography of a panic that had ended before many of its developers were born[31]. Nothing shows the autonomy of the grammar from its geopolitical occasions more clearly: the anxiety about Japan was thirty years dead; the aesthetic reflex survived unweakened.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the mirror, the represented had begun to answer — and the answers complicate any simple story of imposition. Mamoru Oshii's Ghost in the Shell (1995) took the cyberpunk apparatus that Gibson had built from Japanese materials and returned it transformed: a Japanese-made meditation on posthuman identity whose cybernetic protagonist, Major Kusanagi, is precisely the individuated Asian techno-subject that Western cyberpunk had never supplied[32]. The film's global prestige — it directly shaped The Matrix — demonstrated that the aesthetic could be owned by those it depicted; Ueno's mirror, from Part 1, showed its productive face, along with its trap: anime's export success meant Japan was now selling the West the West's own fantasy of Japan, self-Orientalism as industrial strategy[13]. The trap snapped shut with exquisite symbolism in 2017, when Hollywood remade Ghost in the Shell with Scarlett Johansson as the Major — the plot even contriving that her character was originally a Japanese woman whose brain had been transplanted into a white synthetic body, an accidental allegory so perfect that criticism felt redundant: the West had literally extracted the Asian interiority and re-housed it in white shell[33]. The whitewashing controversy that followed belongs to the same moment as the Cloud Atlas protest — the moment when the century-old settlement (Asian material, Western subject) finally became visible as a scandal rather than a default. That visibility is historically new, and Part 4 will argue that the sympathetic Orientalism of The Creator is, among other things, an adaptation to it.

The strongest answers, however, came from writers building futures in which the West is neither audience nor centre. Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem (2008; English translation by Ken Liu, 2014) opens not in a neon arcade but in the Cultural Revolution, roots its physics-epic in Chinese history and Chinese institutions, and populates the species' encounter with the alien almost entirely with Chinese subjects — scientists, engineers, police, ideologues, dreamers — who are neither backdrop nor threat but simply humanity[34]. Its Hugo Award in 2015, the first for any Asian novel, and its tens of millions of copies worldwide constitute the largest single restoration of Asian narrative agency in the genre's history: the future written from Beijing, with the West reading over China's shoulder for a change. Yet even this writing-back was promptly re-processed by the discourse it defied. Western commentary routinely reads Chinese science fiction as national allegory — decoding Liu for state ideology as no one decodes American SF for Americanism — and the broader 'Sinofuturism' conversation, as Gabriele de Seta has argued, functions as an inverse Orientalism: China is again equated with the future, and the future again deployed to defer China's participation in the present — Fabian's denial of coevalness, executed this time through the attribution of futurity rather than backwardness[35]. Even admiration for China's actual technological ascent, in other words, reflexively reaches for the temporal exile that Part 1 identified as the discourse's core operation. (Netflix's 2024 adaptation of Liu, which relocated most of the story to London and diversified the cast away from its Chinese principals, suggests the machinery's digestive power remains excellent.) The diaspora, holding both mirrors at once, produced the era's most self-aware texts: Charles Yu's National Book Award-winning Interior Chinatown (2020) is nothing less than backdrop-isation written from inside the backdrop — its protagonist, Willis Wu, exists within a police-procedural reality where Asian men are cast only as 'Generic Asian Man' or 'Background Oriental Male', and dreams of promotion to 'Kung Fu Guy', the highest role the structure permits[36]. Yu needed no critical vocabulary; he simply wrote the org chart.

Nor was the writing-back confined to prestige literature; the 2010s saw Asian popular cinema simply build the future domestically, at blockbuster scale, and the results are instructive in their normality. Frant Gwo's The Wandering Earth (2019), adapted from Liu, became one of the highest-grossing films in Chinese history with a premise — humanity bolts thrusters to the planet and drives it out of the solar system — whose heroes are Chinese engineers, Chinese astronauts and a Chinese grandfather, and whose emotional register is collectivist sacrifice rather than lone-cowboy mastery: a future not merely populated by Chinese subjects but organised by Chinese values, take them or leave them[37]. Korean cinema and television, riding the hallyu wave, produced space opera (Space Sweepers, 2021) in which a Korean crew of indebted junk-collectors are neither exotic nor emblematic — just working people in orbit, the genre's ordinary humanity finally distributed without racial surcharge[38]. One can debate any of these texts' politics; what is not debatable is their structural achievement: in them, Asian characters possess exactly the unremarkable, unallegorical personhood — the middle register between mastermind and horde — that a century of Western representation could not supply. The point of the comparison is not that Asian self-representation is beyond criticism, but that it proves the deficiency was never in the material. Asia was always representable as subjects. The West's machinery simply was not built to do it.

And India? Part 3 is where the thread becomes a knot, because the 2000s were the decade in which India's technological present became undeniable — and the representational system dealt with it by a division of labour so stark it constitutes a natural experiment run at civilisational scale. While fictional East Asians were being cast as the future's robots, actual South Asians were servicing the present's machines under a regime that demanded their self-erasure as a condition of participation. The sociology of the Indian call centre, now a substantial literature, documents the mechanism with precision. A. Aneesh describes "virtual migration": the Indian worker crosses into Western economic space nightly through the fibre-optic cable, labouring inside American time zones and American workflows while her body remains in Gurgaon — present as function, absent as person[39]. Shehzad Nadeem's Dead Ringers shows workers required to be exactly that — indistinguishable substitutes for the American workers they replaced, equipped with adopted names ('Sean' for Satish), neutralised accents, scripted small talk about weather they have never felt and sports they do not follow[40]. Kiran Mirchandani calls the resulting labour "authenticity work": the nightly production, by Indians, of a plausible non-Indianness, because the service relation runs smoother when the West cannot hear whom it is talking to[41]. Note what this is: the inverse of representation. The East Asian enters Western fantasy as a hypervisible surface with no interior; the South Asian enters Western economy as a functioning interior required to have no surface. One is all image and no voice; the other is all voice and no image — and the voice must be laundered. Nadeem elsewhere reports the term Indian commentators coined for the arrangement — 'cyber-coolie' — in full awareness of its nineteenth-century ancestry: the aggregated, instrumental, infinitely substitutable Asian labourer, now shipped down a cable instead of across an ocean[42]. Meanwhile the one South Asian figure the Western imaginary does celebrate — the immigrant CEO, the Pichai or Nadella — confirms rather than breaks the pattern: he is admitted as the model minority's apex, the excellent steward of technology companies founded by others; admiration for the Indian manager of the present coexists frictionlessly with the unimaginability of an Indian future. Indian speculative fiction has begun writing back against precisely this — Vandana Singh's scientist-heroines, Samit Basu's Delhi-set near-future The City Inside (2022), and, in the popular register, the mythofuturist blockbuster Kalki 2898 AD (2024), which fuses the Mahābhārata with post-apocalyptic SF and simply asserts an Indian future on Indian terms, indifferent to whether the West is watching[43]. Whether these can force a revision of the register-sorting — or will be absorbed, as Liu was, into some 'Indofuturist' variant of the same temporal exile — is one of the open questions on which this essay's final Part will end.

The state of play at the threshold of the AI era, then: the grammar built for Japan had been re-let to China with its structure untouched; the Asian-body-as-machine equation had been refined into a casting convention so standard it operated below intention; the represented had broken into the workshop — Oshii, Liu, Yu and others proving that every element of the apparatus could be reclaimed — and the discourse had shown a disquieting capacity to digest even the reclamations; and South Asia's real technicians remained sealed in the hypo register, audible only in disguise. One further condition had changed, and it set the stage for the mutation to come: the critique itself had gone mainstream. After the Cloud Atlas and Ghost in the Shell controversies, no major production could deploy the old iconography naively; the yellow-peril villain and the yellowface hero were now reputational hazards. A studio wishing to set its AI parable in Asia would need a mode that could survive the scrutiny — a mode that kept the backdrop, kept the aggregation, kept the Western protagonist, but recoded the whole arrangement as tribute. Part 4 examines the film that found it.

Part FourThe AI-Era Mutation: Sympathetic Orientalism and The Creator

Part 3 ended with a prediction in the form of a specification. After the yellowface scandals of the 2010s, the old techno-Orientalist iconography had become a reputational hazard; a major production wishing to set its AI parable in Asia would need a mode that could survive scrutiny — one that kept the backdrop, the aggregation and the Western protagonist, but recoded the arrangement as tribute. In September 2023, at the close of the year in which generative AI had become the West's reigning anxiety, the specification was met. Gareth Edwards's The Creator is a lavish, visually serious science-fiction war film in which the United States, having banned artificial intelligence after a nuclear detonation in Los Angeles, wages a war of extermination against the AI-sheltering lands of "New Asia" — a pan-Asian expanse of rice paddies, Buddhist iconography and floating villages — using an orbital super-weapon called NOMAD; an American ex-special-forces protagonist, Joshua, is sent to find and destroy the AI creator's ultimate weapon, which proves to be a robot child, Alphie; he defects, protects the child, and dies redeeming himself[44]. The film divided its audiences in a manner so diagnostic, so nearly experimental, that it furnishes this essay's culminating case: it was attacked, simultaneously and with equal conviction, as anti-Western propaganda and as anti-Asian Orientalism. Our purpose in this Part is to show that both attacks are tracking real properties of the film — that they are measurements taken along two different axes — and that distinguishing those axes yields the general account of what techno-Orientalism has become in the age of AI.

Take the film's surface first, because the surface is genuinely new. Every prior phase of this history assigned Asia a negative or at best neutral moral charge: the Yellow Peril's demonic masterminds, cyberpunk's soulless corporate future, the China-era's totalitarian dystopias. The Creator inverts the charge completely. Its Asia is warm, communal, spiritual, victimised and good; its AI 'simulants' — who wear Asian faces almost without exception — are gentle monks, loving parents, brave villagers; its United States is a genocidal aggressor whose soldiers menace weeping farmers, execute prisoners and incinerate villages from orbit. The film's visual rhetoric of atrocity is borrowed wholesale from the Vietnam War film — hovercraft over rice paddies, napalm skies, the ground-level terror of peasants before American firepower — and critics recognised the lineage instantly; the film is, among other things, Apocalypse Now re-run with the sympathies formally reversed[45]. Even the imperial idiom is quoted verbatim: Joshua's handler, watching his loyalty waver, warns him — "Don't go native on me" — a line that renders the film's colonial subtext as text with almost scholarly helpfulness[45][46]. On the axis of moral valence, then, the film's position is unambiguous: it punishes the West and canonises Asia. Conservative American criticism read this axis, read it accurately, and was duly outraged: Movieguide's review found "an incredibly overt, bizarre Anti-American, politically correct, racist viewpoint", protesting that "all but one of the white American characters are despicable villains and all the Asians, robots and androids are really nice and good"[47]. As a description of the film's moral casting, this is simply correct.

But now take the second axis, and watch the same film transform. Ask not whom does the film love? but whom does the film individuate? — not who is good? but who acts, chooses, develops, decides? The answers are devastatingly familiar. "New Asia" is one undifferentiated territory in which Thai islands, Vietnamese deltas, Himalayan monasteries, Japanese faces and Korean signage compose a single interchangeable Asianness — the exclusion era's aggregated 'Asiatic', the production designer's Firefly, now stretched across half a continent and rebranded as homage[48]. Its inhabitants, human and simulant alike, are kind, brave, photogenic — and generic: they shelter the Western protagonist, they suffer beautifully, they die in illustrative masses, and not one of them is granted an interior conflict, a change of mind, an arc. The film's Asian-featured characters with the most screen presence conform precisely to the old templates: Maya, the protagonist's Asian wife, is loved, lost and mourned — a motivating memory more than a person; Harun, the simulant resistance leader played by Ken Watanabe, exists to fight, explain and nobly die for the protagonist's education; Alphie, the robot child in whom the film vests its whole future, is a mute-then-childlike object of protection whose function is to be carried, endangered and wept over. Every consequential decision in the film — to defect, to protect, to sacrifice, to destroy NOMAD — is taken by Joshua. Asia, at maximum moral prestige, supplies what it has supplied since 1982: the setting, the stakes and the corpses; the West supplies the subject. Siddhant Adlakha's verdict in JoySauce identified the mechanism exactly: by having robots "almost entirely stand in for Asian peoples", without mounting a cinematic argument for those robots' own interiority, the film produces a dynamic he called "immediately brutalizing and xenophobic" — Asian-coded beings exist to be destroyed at scale, pitied, and avenged by a Westerner[48]. The Alien/Asian equation of Part 3 here reaches its logical terminus: an entire Asian population literally cast as machines — the Yellow Peril's biological robots of the 1870s, upgraded to actual robots, and this time we are asked to love them[29][48].

The film's handling of Asian religion completes the structural audit, and folds in the South Asian thread with an irony almost too neat to be accidental. The Creator's AI civilisation is saturated in sacred iconography — robot monks in saffron, temple architecture, funerary rites, the machinery of Buddhist and Hindu devotion redeployed as production design — and its supreme being, the unseen architect of the new machine life, is called "Nirmata": the Sanskrit nirmātā (निर्माता), 'maker' or 'creator'[44]. South Asia is thus present in the film at the level of the sacred vocabulary — its language supplies the god-term, its religious aesthetics supply the AI sublime — while remaining absent, as ever, from the film's technological agency and virtually absent from its cast of speaking persons. This is Part 1's register-sorting executed with textbook fidelity in 2023: East Asian faces for the machines, South-East Asian landscapes for the territory, South Asian spirituality for the transcendence — every region of Asia contributing its assigned raw material to a composite that no Asian controls. Adlakha's review noted the mechanism precisely: the film adopts "elements of Hinduism, Buddhism and Asian cultures to suggest something profound" about its AI, spiritual shorthand standing in for the interiority the script never writes[48]. The mystic-past register, in other words, has not been retired by the sympathetic mutation; it has been fused with the soulless-future register into a single figure — the holy machine — in which both of the discourse's temporal exiles finally meet. The Orient is at last permitted to be the future and the past simultaneously; the one thing it still cannot be is the present.

The bidirectional criticism, then, is not a curiosity of polarised reception; it is the two-axes structure of the film registering on instruments that could each read only one axis. The conservative reading measured moral valence and found Asia elevated; the culture-critical reading measured representational structure and found Asia instrumentalised; both are right, because the film really does run these two operations simultaneously — and the possibility of running them simultaneously is itself the finding. If 'pro-Asian' and 'anti-Asian' were positions on a single scale, the two readings would be contradictory and at least one would have to be wrong. They are not contradictory, because moral valence and narrative agency are independent variables — a text can set the first to maximum warmth while holding the second at zero. That a blockbuster did precisely this, and that each half of the culture could see only the axis it was calibrated for, confirms the axes are real and genuinely distinct. And there is a further, sharper implication. The very fact that the film reads as 'about Asia' at all — that audiences on all sides instantly decoded robot-kind as Asian-kind — demonstrates how legible the racial coding remains beneath the science-fiction alibi. The film never says its machines are Asia; it merely dresses them in Asia, locates them in Asia, and scores them with Asia — and everyone understood. A coding that requires no statement is a coding that has become infrastructure[1][48].

We proposed at the outset to call this mode sympathetic Orientalism, and we can now define it with precision: the representation of a racialised group with maximal positive moral valence and minimal narrative agency — virtue conferred, subjecthood withheld. The genealogy is old; the eighteenth century called its version the 'noble savage', which ascribed to colonised peoples a natural goodness superior to European corruption while denying them precisely the capacities — complexity, contradiction, authorship of their own story — that constitute personhood in narrative. Sympathetic Orientalism is the noble savage rebuilt for the AI age: the noble simulant, serene, spiritual, self-sacrificing, adorable — and structurally identical to the backdrop populations of every earlier phase. Said, recall from Part 1, insisted that Orientalism embraced its objects with fascination, sympathy and even love, and that affection was no alibi[2]; The Creator is the cinematic proof of the theorem. Indeed the sympathetic mode is in one respect more efficient than its hostile predecessors: hostility invites resistance, but tribute pre-empts it. A film that flatters Asia morally while consuming it structurally has metabolised the critique of Parts 2 and 3 — it can cite its own reverence as the answer to any objection, and its defenders can honestly point to the axis on which the reverence is real. This is why the mutation appeared when it did: after the scandals made hostile and appropriative modes unusable, the machinery — which, as we have seen at every stage, conserves structure and negotiates surface — negotiated the surface again. Sympathetic Orientalism is still Orientalism, because Orientalism was never, at its core, a sentiment. It is an allocation: of subjecthood to one side and scenery to the other. The sentiment is the paint[2][48].

One refinement of the film deserves separate notice, because it shows the structure's independence from its most-discussed marker. Joshua is played by John David Washington; the redeemer at the centre of The Creator's saviour arc is Black. The casting sidesteps the literal 'white saviour' label — and thereby demonstrates that the label was always naming the wrong property. The saviour structure is positional, not phenotypic: it consists in the asymmetry between the one who decides and the many who are decided about, between the character who develops and the population that occasions his development. A non-white actor in the position leaves the position intact — Western subject, Asian scenery — while inoculating the film against the standard critique's standard vocabulary. The lesson generalises, and it is one of this essay's central claims: auditing representation by the demographics of its cast measures the surface; auditing it by the distribution of narrative agency measures the structure. The first audit can be passed by casting. Only the second detects backdrop-isation — in The Creator, in Firefly's affectionate emptiness, in Ex Machina's silent Kyoko, in every text where the numbers look progressive and the grammar has not moved[44][48].

Where does the trope go from here? The geopolitical engine, at least, is unmistakable: the anxiety that filled the grammar with Japan in 1982 and China in 2012 is now specifically an AI anxiety. The semiconductor export controls of 2022 framed advanced computation itself as a strategic substance to be denied to Asia; the release of DeepSeek's reasoning models in early 2025 — greeted in American commentary, with weary inevitability, as a "Sputnik moment" — re-ran the 1980s semiconductor panic almost line by line: the rival's achievement read not as evidence of creativity but of theft, subsidy, or inhuman efficiency of scale, the hypo and hyper poles oscillating in real time on cable news[49]. All the precursor conditions of a new representational cycle are therefore in place, and its two modes are already distinguishable. The security mode — Chinese AI as swarm, as surveillance hive, as the soulless super-intelligence of think-tank nightmares — is the Yellow Peril's direct descendant and needs no elaboration. The sympathetic mode is the one this Part has anatomised, and its next iterations are predictable: as debates over AI consciousness and rights intensify, expect the suffering machine to be racialised again — the enslaved robot with the Asian face, pitied by Western protagonists who once again reserve the deciding to themselves. Meanwhile the South Asian thread completes the pattern into the AI age with dispiriting neatness: the world's largest pools of data-annotation and content-moderation labour — the millions of clicks that actually taught the machines to see — sit in India, Kenya and the Philippines, invisible in every Western AI imaginary; the hypo register absorbs the AI era's real Asian labour exactly as it absorbed the call centre's, while the hyper register runs its fantasies of East Asian machine-minds. One grammar; two tenses; now three centuries[1][12][49].

The four-part historical argument is now complete, and its findings can be stated in a paragraph. Techno-Orientalism is not a stereotype but a machinery, and the machinery has five conserved components: aggregation (Asia as mass, horde, skyline, 'New Asia'); instrumentality (Asian figures as means — labour, atmosphere, motivation — rather than ends); denial of coevalness (Asia exiled from the shared present, backwards into mystic antiquity or forwards into soulless or sacred futurity); register-sorting (East Asia assigned the technological future, South Asia the spiritual past and the invisible service present); and the reservation of narrative agency to the West. Across a century and a half the machinery has changed its surface completely — villain to victim, menace to muse, hatred to homage — while every structural component has survived every change. The two-axes analysis explains both the persistence and the camouflage: because critics and audiences instinctively audit valence, a discourse that repaints its valence purchases invisibility for its structure. The repair, when it comes, will therefore not come from warmer portrayals, more reverent backdrops or more diverse casting within the old allocation. It will come — it is already coming, as Part 3's other side of the mirror showed — from the redistribution of the only asset that was ever really at stake: the position of the subject. Oshii's Major, Liu's physicists, Yu's Willis Wu, Singh's scientists, the ordinary Korean salvage crew of a Netflix space opera — these are not better images of Asia; they are Asian persons in the tense the discourse always denied them, the present, making the futures. The test for any Western text that claims to have escaped techno-Orientalism is correspondingly simple, and we offer it as this essay's practical conclusion, in the form of three questions: Who is individuated? Who decides? And when the Asian character suffers, does the story's camera grieve with them — or does it check, first, what their suffering has taught the man from the West?

Part FiveGlossary

Terms are listed alphabetically. Cross-references to other glossary entries are given in italics. Terms coined or given specialised senses within this essay are marked †.

Aggregation † (as used here). The representational operation by which Asian persons are rendered as an undifferentiated mass — horde, swarm, crowd, skyline, service class, 'New Asia' — rather than as individuated persons. Identified in Part 1 as the Yellow Peril's founding gesture and tracked through every subsequent phase. The middle register of ordinary personhood, between demonic mastermind and faceless multitude, is precisely what aggregation cannot represent.

Alien/Asian . Stephen Hong Sohn's term for the speculative-fiction equation by which the racialised Asian figure stands in for the technological non-human as such — the robot, the clone, the replicant, the AI. The casting convention that gives androids Asian features (Ex Machina's Kyoko, Cloud Atlas's fabricants, The Creator's simulants) is its practical expression.

Asiatic Barred Zone . The territory — stretching from the Middle East through South Asia to the Pacific islands — from which the United States Immigration Act of 1917 barred immigration. Cited in Part 1 as evidence that the legal category 'Asiatic' aggregated East and South Asia into a single excludable mass decades before popular culture did the same.

Authenticity work . Kiran Mirchandani's term for the labour performed by Indian call-centre workers in producing a plausible non-Indianness — adopted names, neutralised accents, scripted cultural small talk — as a condition of serving Western customers. Discussed in Part 3 as the inverse of representation: presence granted only through self-erasure.

Backdrop-isation † . This essay's term for the operation by which a culture is converted into scenery: present throughout a text as signage, cuisine, crowd, iconography and mood, while absent from the text's structure of persons — no consequential choices, interior conflicts or arcs are assigned to its members. Distinct from villainisation and fully compatible with affection; identified as the load-bearing operation of techno-Orientalism in every phase.

Coevalness, denial of . Johannes Fabian's term for the discursive refusal to allow an observed culture to inhabit the same present as the observer. Classical Orientalism denies coevalness backwards (Asia as past); techno-Orientalism denies it forwards (Asia as future). The shared present — the tense of actual persons — is what both modes withhold.

Cyberpunk . The science-fiction mode codified in the early 1980s by Blade Runner and Neuromancer: near-future, urban, corporate-dominated, technologically saturated and noir in affect. Its canonical mise-en-scène — neon ideograms, permanent rain, Asian street crowds — made it techno-Orientalism's principal twentieth-century vehicle.

Cyber-coolie . Term circulating in Indian commentary and labour scholarship (documented by Shehzad Nadeem) for the outsourced Indian technology worker, deliberately invoking the nineteenth-century 'coolie': aggregated, instrumental, infinitely substitutable Asian labour — now transmitted by cable rather than shipped by sea.

Discourse (Foucauldian sense). A self-sustaining system of statements, images, institutions and practices that produces its own object of knowledge and licenses action upon it. Said's claim that Orientalism is a discourse — not a correctable set of individual prejudices — underwrites this essay's claim that techno-Orientalism is a machinery rather than a stereotype.

Dragon lady / lotus blossom . The paired stereotypes into which Yellow Peril-era fiction sorted Asian femininity: seduction weaponised and submission perfected. Neither is a person; both are apparatus. Identified in Part 1 as the gendered template on which later Asian-featured androids and gynoids draw.

Fabricant . In Cloud Atlas (2012), a genetically manufactured Asian service worker, cloned in series, worked to exhaustion and recycled. Discussed in Part 3 as the techno-Orientalist inheritance rendered literal: aggregation, instrumentality and the gendered template in a single figure.

Hallyu . The 'Korean wave' of globally successful South Korean popular culture. Context for Part 3's discussion of Korean science fiction's domestically built futures.

High-tech Orientalism . Wendy Hui Kyong Chun's term for cyberpunk's characteristic pleasure: the Western reader's or viewer's exhilarating navigation of a disorienting, Asian-signed technological space, in which Western vulnerability is converted into enjoyment through guaranteed narrative mastery.

Hypo/hypertechnological oscillation . From Roh, Huang and Niu's definition of techno-Orientalism: the imagining of Asians as simultaneously hypertechnological (masters of the machine, avatars of the future) and hypotechnological (imitators, cheap labour, incapable of creation). The poles cooperate rather than conflict: both deny ordinary creative personhood. The essay assigns East Asia's stereotype primarily to the hyper pole and South Asia's to the hypo pole (register-sorting).

Instrumentality † (as used here). The representational operation by which Asian figures are imagined as means — labour, atmosphere, motivation, raw material — rather than as ends in themselves. Second of the five conserved components of the techno-Orientalist machinery identified in Part 4.

Japan panic . The episode of American economic dread (c. 1979–1992) occasioned by Japanese industrial ascendancy, whose cultural processing Morley and Robins analysed in the chapter that coined 'techno-Orientalism'. Part 2's subject.

Latent / manifest Orientalism . Said's distinction between the near-unconscious substrate of assumptions about the East (latent: timeless, sensual, despotic, irrational, feminine) and the explicit statements, policies and representations in which it surfaces at a given moment (manifest). The manifest changes; the latent persists. Ancestor of this essay's two-axes analysis.

Model minority . The stereotype of Asians as diligent, uncomplaining, technically able and interchangeable. Part 3 argues this is structurally praise for a good machine, connecting the social stereotype to the Alien/Asian casting convention.

Moral valence † . The first axis of the essay's two-axes analysis: the moral charge a text assigns to a group — villain, victim, saint. Independent of narrative agency. The Creator sets Asia's moral valence to maximum warmth.

Narrative agency / representational structure † . The second axis: the distribution of subjecthood — who is individuated, who decides, who develops, whose interiority the text explores. A text may elevate a group's moral valence while withholding narrative agency entirely; this combination defines sympathetic Orientalism.

Noble savage . The eighteenth-century figure of the colonised person as naturally good, uncorrupted and simple — virtue ascribed, complexity and authorship denied. Identified in Part 4 as sympathetic Orientalism's direct ancestor; The Creator's gentle simulants are 'the noble savage rebuilt for the AI age'.

Orientalism . In Said's analysis: the Western discourse that produces 'the Orient' as an object of knowledge and site of intervention — "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient". Not reducible to hostility; embraces fascination, sympathy and love without ceasing to allocate subjecthood asymmetrically.

Oriental style . Jane Chi Hyun Park's term for Asianness as a detachable visual register connoting technological futurity, deployable by Hollywood independently of any Asian character's presence. The mechanism by which the cyberpunk aesthetic became portable (Part 2).

Register-sorting † . This essay's term for the discourse's selective geography: the assignment of East Asia to the technological future (hyper pole) and South Asia to the mystic past and invisible service present (hypo pole), within a shared denial of coevalness. Fourth conserved component of the machinery; traced from the 1917 Barred Zone to The Creator's composite Asia.

Said, Edward W. (1935–2003) . Palestinian-American literary theorist whose Orientalism (1978) founded colonial-discourse analysis and supplies this essay's baseline framework.

Self-Orientalism . The adoption, elaboration or strategic performance of Orientalist stereotypes by the stereotyped culture itself — as in Japan's export of its own cyberpunk image (Ueno's mirror), or state-branded 'Cool Japan'. Discussed in Part 3 alongside its critical potential and its commercial trap.

Simulant . In The Creator (2023), a humanoid AI being; simulants wear Asian faces almost without exception, making the film's machine population an Asian-coded population (Alien/Asian).

Sinofuturism . The discourse equating China with the future — in commentary, art and criticism. Gabriele de Seta analyses it as inverse Orientalism: futurity deployed, as backwardness once was, to defer China's participation in the present (coevalness).

Sympathetic Orientalism † . This essay's name for the AI-era mutation exemplified by The Creator: representation of a racialised group with maximal positive moral valence and minimal narrative agency — virtue conferred, subjecthood withheld. The essay's summary verdict: sympathetic Orientalism is still Orientalism, because Orientalism is an allocation, not a sentiment.

Techno-Orientalism . Coined by Morley and Robins (1995); developed by Roh, Huang and Niu (2015) as "the phenomenon of imagining Asia and Asians in hypo- or hypertechnological terms in cultural productions and political discourse". This essay's subject, analysed as a machinery of five conserved components: aggregation, instrumentality, denial of coevalness, register-sorting, and Western reservation of narrative agency.

Temporal inversion † . This essay's term for the pivotal late-twentieth-century manoeuvre by which East Asia, long figured as the West's primitive past, was refigured as its dehumanised future — hierarchy preserved by relocating humanity from capability to interiority. The hinge between classical Orientalism and techno-Orientalism (Part 1–2).

Two-axes analysis † . The essay's central original contribution: the distinction between moral valence and narrative agency as independent axes of representation. Dissolves the apparent paradox of texts criticised simultaneously as 'anti-Western' and 'anti-Asian'; explains how valence-repainting purchases invisibility for structural continuity.

Virtual migration . A. Aneesh's term for labour that crosses borders while the labourer does not: the Indian worker present in Western economic space as function, absent as person. Part 3's key concept for the South Asian hypo register.

Whitewashing . The casting of white actors in roles written as, or sourced from, characters of colour — e.g. the 2017 Ghost in the Shell remake. Distinct from yellowface (white actors made up to appear Asian, e.g. Cloud Atlas's Neo-Seoul segment), though both reallocate Asian subject positions to white performers.

Yellow Peril . The late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Euro-American panic imagining Asia as a demographic, economic and military menace; its fiction (Shiel, London, Rohmer) forged the image-repertoire — aggregation, the mechanical horde, the scientist-despot — that techno-Orientalism inherited.

Zaibatsu . Japanese family-held industrial conglomerate (pre-1945 form). In cyberpunk, the standard figure of the Asian corporation as feudal, collective and immortal — Gibson's "hives with cybernetic memories"; Cyberpunk 2077's Arasaka.

Part SixNotes

The main text of Parts 1–4 was written to be self-sufficient, and no footnote markers interrupt it; the notes below supply supplementary and qualifying material, keyed to the Part and passage they concern.

[N1] (Part 1, on 'Orient' and 'Asia'). Said's Orientalism concerns principally the Middle East and the Islamic world as constructed by British, French and later American scholarship; its extension to East and South Asian representation is a development of the subsequent literature, including the techno-Orientalism scholarship this essay follows. We use 'the Orient' only when reporting the discourse's own vocabulary, and 'Asia' otherwise. Nothing in the argument depends on treating 'Asia' as a natural unit — indeed the essay's register-sorting thesis is precisely that the discourse's own aggregations ('Asiatic', 'New Asia') are constructions with legal and cinematic histories.

[N2] (Part 1, on the Knackfuss engraving). The 1895 allegorical image distributed by Kaiser Wilhelm II — Völker Europas, wahrt eure heiligsten Güter ('Peoples of Europe, guard your most sacred possessions'), executed by Hermann Knackfuss after the Kaiser's own sketch — depicts the Archangel Michael exhorting armoured female personifications of the European nations towards a gathering storm-cloud in the East, within which sits a Buddha figure. The seated Buddha as the icon of menace is worth registering: the peril was figured from the outset in religious as much as racial terms, anticipating the sacred-Asia imagery whose sympathetic inversion Part 4 traces in The Creator.

[N3] (Part 1, on the Page Act). The Page Act of 1875 nominally barred contract labourers and women imported 'for the purposes of prostitution' from 'China, Japan, or any Oriental country'; in enforcement it functioned as a near-total bar on Chinese women's immigration, with consequences for family formation in Chinese America that lasted generations. It precedes the better-known Exclusion Act of 1882 and is the reason Part 1 dates the gendering of the Yellow Peril archive to the very beginning of the exclusion era.

[N4] (Part 1, on 'the yellow peril incarnate'). Rohmer's celebrated description of Fu-Manchu — "the yellow peril incarnate in one man" — appears in the opening chapters of The Mystery of Dr. Fu-Manchu (1913), in Nayland Smith's breathless catalogue of the Doctor's attributes ("Imagine a person, tall, lean and feline... with all the cruel cunning of an entire Eastern race, accumulated in one giant intellect"). The passage is itself a perfect specimen of aggregation: an 'entire race' compressed into a single figure.

[N5] (Part 2, on the Toshiba incident). The 1987 sledgehammer episode followed revelations that a Toshiba subsidiary had sold the Soviet Union milling technology useful for quieting submarine propellers. The congressmen's target, however, was a consumer radio-cassette player — an object with no connection to the violation — which is precisely why the image belongs to the history of representation rather than of trade enforcement: the gesture punished 'Japanese technology' as such.

[N6] (Part 2, on Cityspeak). Blade Runner's street language, 'Cityspeak', is described in Deckard's voiceover as a "mishmash of Japanese, Spanish, German, what have you"; Edward James Olmos, who played Gaff, devised much of it himself, incorporating Hungarian among other languages. The polyglot gutter-language spoken by the future's crowds — against the standard English of every deciding character — is a small, telling instance of the film's allocation of interiority.

[N7] (Part 2, on Dick's novel). Philip K. Dick's Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968) is set in a post-war San Francisco depopulated by radioactive dust; its anxieties are demographic emptiness and the erosion of empathy, not Asian saturation. The comparison isolates the Asianisation of the film's Los Angeles as a deliberate 1982 design decision, contemporary with the Japan panic, rather than an inheritance from the source material.

[N8] (Part 3, on Looper's Shanghai). The relocation of Looper's future scenes from Paris to Shanghai followed Chinese co-production financing (DMG Entertainment), and additional Shanghai footage was included in the Chinese release cut. The economics deserve note: by the 2010s, Asia was no longer merely the West's image of the future but a decisive market for it — a material shift underlying the era's softened moral valences that Part 4 analyses.

[N9] (Part 3, on the Netflix adaptation). Netflix's 3 Body Problem (2024) retains the Cultural Revolution frame but relocates the present-day narrative to Britain and recasts most principals as a multinational Oxford cohort. Whatever its merits as adaptation, the pattern is the one Part 3 names 'digestive': the largest restoration of Chinese narrative agency in genre history was, within a decade, re-centred on the Anglosphere for global distribution.

[N10] (Part 4, on 'Nirmata'). Sanskrit nirmātā (निर्माता), agent noun from nir-√mā 'to construct, fashion, create': 'maker, builder, creator'. The word is standard in modern South Asian languages for a film producer, among other senses. The film's choice of a Sanskrit-derived god-term for its AI creator — in a setting whose speaking cast is overwhelmingly East and South-East Asian-featured — is the essay's clearest single instance of South Asia present as sacred vocabulary while absent as agency.

[N11] (Part 4, on the reception evidence). The bidirectional-criticism argument does not require that the quoted outlets be representative of all conservative or all culture-critical reception, only that both readings were publicly articulated, each tracking a real textual property. Defences of the film — e.g. readings of it as anti-imperialist allegory in the libertarian press — constitute a third position, which the two-axes analysis accommodates without strain: they are valence-readings that approve what Movieguide's valence-reading deplores, while leaving the structural axis unexamined.

[N12] (Part 4, on data-annotation labour). The claim that AI-era annotation and moderation labour concentrates in India, Kenya and the Philippines refers to the industry structure documented in reporting and scholarship on firms such as Sama and Appen and on the RLHF supply chain generally; the point in the argument is structural — the labour is real, Asian (and African), present-tense and invisible in the Western AI imaginary — not a precise census of the sector.

[N13] (General, on 'the West'). Throughout, 'the West' names a discursive position — the subject-position from which the representations under analysis are produced and consumed — not a demographic claim about audiences or makers, many of whom (as the Cyberpunk 2077 case shows) need not be American or British for the grammar to operate. The essay's own argument that a Black protagonist can occupy the 'Western saviour' position (Part 4) applies the same principle: these are positions in a structure, not skins.
